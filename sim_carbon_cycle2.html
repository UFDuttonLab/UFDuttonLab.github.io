Key takeaways
  1. Carbon is conserved. Burning moves carbon out of the ground; every tonne lost from fossil fuels shows up in the air, the ocean, plants or soils. The bottom chart always balances.
  2. Fossil fuels add old carbon to the fast cycle. Carbon locked away for millions of years returns to the atmosphere in decades, faster than natural sinks can absorb it.
  3. The ocean is the biggest sink but it is slow. It takes up carbon over decades to centuries, and the more it absorbs, the less it can take. The extra CO₂ also makes seawater more acidic.
  4. Stopping emissions does not reset the clock. When burning stops, CO₂ falls slowly and settles well above the preindustrial 280 ppm. The extra carbon stays in the fast cycle for thousands of years.

Gross land fluxes after IPCC AR6 WG1 Fig. 5.12. Sinks start at today’s values (ocean ~2.5, land ~3.3 Gt C/yr; Friedlingstein et al. 2023, Global Carbon Budget); the land sink comes from CO₂ fertilization and weakens as plant and soil stocks catch up. Long-term warming = 3 °C per doubling of CO₂ (equilibrium climate sensitivity, IPCC AR6), CO₂ only.
